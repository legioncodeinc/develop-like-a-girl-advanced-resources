# Define the problem, the customer, and the job

Fills sections 1, 2 and 3 of the [template](../references/templates/product-strategy.md). Questions: Batches 1 and 2.

## The problem (section 1)

1. Get one specific story: one person, what happened, what they tried, what it cost. Shape Up calls this the baseline you test any solution against (Shape Up, Write the Pitch).
2. Ask what they do today. Include "nothing" and "a spreadsheet" as real answers.
3. Ask how the owner knows. Write the honest answer. "I have this problem myself" is evidence for one person, not a market.
4. If demand is untested, add it to section 8 as a desirability assumption. A founder's post-mortem shows "sounds interesting" from friends is politeness, not demand (Mind the Product, Three Products Three Failures (2026)).
5. Do not spend the whole session proving the problem is real. SVPG argues the hard part is usually solving it better than the alternatives (SVPG, Build to Learn FAQ (2026)). Get enough to write the story, log the doubt, move on.

## Who it is for (section 2)

1. Pick one best-fit segment, described by situation and need. Dunford's best-fit customers are the ones who care most about your unique value (April Dunford, Positioning Quickstart (2021)). You may come back and sharpen this after positioning.
2. Ask if the user, the buyer, and the approver are the same person. In B2B, a product can solve a real problem and still fail when the buyer has no reason to act or your channel cannot reach them (Mind the Product, Useful Is Not Enough (2026)). If they differ, name each one.
3. Write at least one "Not for (yet)" segment. Choosing who you ignore is part of strategy (Lenny, Strategy Blocks (2025)).
4. Keep the ICP to a short summary. If the owner is not the customer, the buyer is unclear, or the owner asks, offer an owner-designated customer research method (unavailable as a Advanced specialist) (see [handoffs](../references/handoffs.md)) and link its dossier instead of copying it.

## The job to be done (section 3)

1. Draft "When {situation}, they want to {motivation}, so they can {outcome}" from the owner's own words. Offer it as the (Recommended) answer and let them edit.
2. List the top pains and gains. The Value Proposition Canvas frames a segment by jobs, pains, and gains, and says fit is only a claim until customers confirm it (Strategyzer, Value Proposition Canvas).
3. Check the job is a need, not a feature. "They want a dashboard" is a solution in disguise; ask what the dashboard would let them do (Product Talk, Opportunity Solution Trees (2023)).

## Done when

- Section 1 has a specific story and an honest "how we know".
- Section 2 names one segment, the payer, and at least one "not for".
- Section 3 has one job sentence the owner agreed to.
