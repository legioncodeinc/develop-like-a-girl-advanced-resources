## 1. What a product strategy is

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| Strategy is how you plan to reach a goal; tactics are the details. Product strategy decides which problems to solve; discovery finds solutions; delivery builds them | (SVPG, Product Strategy Overview (2020)) |
| Strategy needs four things: hard choices (focus), insights, turning insights into action, and active management | (SVPG, Product Strategy Overview (2020)) |
| Product strategy sits between mission/vision and the plan. It forces a choice about scarce resources | (Lenny, Strategy Blocks (2025)) |
| A good strategy has 3 to 5 focus areas ("pillars"), several areas that are explicitly NOT the focus, and the reasons for both | (Lenny, Strategy Blocks (2025)) |
| Score candidate opportunity areas on expected impact, certainty of impact, clarity of levers, and uniqueness of levers. Qualitative scores from team debate are an acceptable fallback when data is missing | (Lenny, Strategy Blocks (2025)) |
| A "winning aspiration" can be written as a future news headline about how customers' lives got better and the company grew | (Lenny, Strategy Blocks (2025)) |
| Product strategy is what picks the problem. Usually product leaders do this | (SVPG, Build to Learn FAQ (2026)) |
| Root cause of output-over-outcome is the wish for predictability. Roadmaps and PRDs feed the belief that we know more than we do | (SVPG, Predictability vs Outcomes (2026)), (SVPG, Strong Opinions Loosely Held (2026)) |
| A strategy should be traceable: every decision should point back to the evidence and objective it rests on, so when evidence breaks you can see what falls with it | (Mind the Product, Three Products Three Failures (2026)) |

**Bets count.** Strategy Blocks recommends 3 to 5 pillars for a company or team (Lenny, Strategy Blocks (2025)). This method uses 2 to 4 bets for a new product. Treat 2 to 4 as this strategy method's house rule for a v1 strategy, not as a sourced number.

## 2. Problem, job, and who it is for

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| Always present a problem with a solution. The best problem statement is one specific story showing why today's way does not work | (Shape Up, Write the Pitch) |
| Describe one customer segment: the jobs they try to get done, the pains they hit, and the gains they want. "Fit" is a claim until customers confirm it | (Strategyzer, Value Proposition Canvas) |
| Opportunities (needs, pains, desires) are not solutions. Test an opportunity so it is not a solution in disguise | (Product Talk, Opportunity Solution Trees (2023)) |
| Knowing the problem is real is usually not the hard part; solving it better than the alternatives is. Do not spend all your time re-validating the problem | (SVPG, Build to Learn FAQ (2026)), (SVPG, Strong Opinions Loosely Held (2026)) |
| CONFLICT: one founder's post-mortem says the first failure was never validating demand at all; "interesting" was politeness, not a signal | (Mind the Product, Three Products Three Failures (2026)) |
| In B2B a real problem can still fail if the buyer has no reason to act, the packaging is wrong, or the channel cannot reach the buyer: "product-market-buyer-channel fit" | (Mind the Product, Useful Is Not Enough (2026)) |
| The more important "why" is why people use or stop using the product | (SVPG, Strong Opinions Loosely Held (2026)) |

**Reading the conflict.** SVPG's view applies when leaders have already picked a well-known problem. The Mind the Product post applies to a brand-new idea with no customers yet. For a raw idea, this strategy method treats "does anyone have this problem badly enough" as an assumption to log and test, not a settled fact.

## 3. Positioning and differentiation

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| Positioning defines how a product is the best at something a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about. It is not messaging, a tagline, or a vision | (April Dunford, Positioning Quickstart (2021)) |
| Five parts, in this order: competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, best-fit customers, market category | (April Dunford, Positioning Quickstart (2021)) |
| Competitive alternatives means what customers would do if you did not exist, including "do nothing" or a spreadsheet. Avoid "phantom competitors" you never meet in deals | (April Dunford, Positioning Quickstart (2021)) |
| Starting in an existing market category is more common and cheaper than creating a new one | (April Dunford, Positioning Quickstart (2021)) |
| The fill-in-the-blanks positioning statement is "pointless but potentially dangerous" because it assumes one answer per blank | (April Dunford, Positioning Quickstart (2021)) |
| Positioning is a company decision (pricing, sales, roadmap), not just marketing. Re-check it on a schedule, about every six months | (Mind the Product, Dunford Positioning (2026)) |
| A solution must be better than the alternatives by enough that the customer chooses to switch | (SVPG, Build to Learn and Build to Earn (2026)) |

**Small conflict.** The 2021 article describes the repositioned product as a "Microsoft Access killer" database (April Dunford, Positioning Quickstart (2021)); the 2026 podcast summary calls it a spreadsheet tool (Mind the Product, Dunford Positioning (2026)). The lesson is the same either way. Do not quote the product type.

## 4. North-star metric and inputs

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| The North Star Metric is a single rate, count, or ratio that represents your product strategy and is a leading indicator of lasting business results and customer value | (Amplitude, North Star checklist (undated)) |
| Inputs are usually 3 to 5 complementary factors that most directly move the North Star and that teams can change with daily work | (Amplitude, North Star checklist (undated)), (Amplitude, Good and Bad North Stars (date conflict)) |
| Checklist: shows customer value, represents strategy, leading indicator of revenue, something you can influence, plain language, measurable, not a vanity metric | (Amplitude, North Star checklist (undated)) |
| Revenue, MRR and ARPU are lagging. Registered users and DAU say nothing about value | (Amplitude, Good and Bad North Stars (date conflict)), (Amplitude, North Star checklist (undated)) |
| Name the "game" the product plays (the checklist lists attention, transaction and productivity games) before picking a metric | (Amplitude, North Star checklist (undated)) |
| Each metric and input needs a name and a definition. Thresholds should be aspirational and achievable; do not chase the perfect number | (Amplitude, North Star checklist (undated)) |
| Do not reject a good concept just because you cannot measure it with today's tools | (Amplitude, North Star checklist (undated)) |
| The North Star changes when strategy changes; Amplitude changed its own at least three times | (Amplitude, Good and Bad North Stars (date conflict)) |
| Connect work to the North Star through bets, which can be big or small and are always outcome-focused | (Amplitude, North Star checklist (undated)) |
| Product outcomes (a customer behavior) suit teams better than business outcomes (revenue) or traction metrics (one feature) | (Product Talk, Opportunity Solution Trees (2023)) |

**House rule.** This method uses exactly 3 input metrics. That sits inside the sourced 3 to 5 range.

## 5. V1 scope and the NOT-in-v1 list

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| A pitch has five parts: problem, appetite (how much time it is worth), solution, rabbit holes, and no-gos (things deliberately left out) | (Shape Up, Write the Pitch) |
| Appetite is a constraint: "if we only care enough to spend two weeks, what solution fits?" | (Shape Up, Write the Pitch) |
| Build to learn (prototypes in discovery) is different from build to earn (commercial-quality delivery) | (SVPG, Build to Learn and Build to Earn (2026)) |
| A high-fidelity prototype is not a product. Real products carry reliability, security, scale, compliance and operations work | (SVPG, Prototypes vs Products (2025)) |
| Strategy should name what is explicitly not the focus | (Lenny, Strategy Blocks (2025)) |

## 6. Risks, assumptions, and the riskiest assumption test

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| Five assumption types: desirability, viability, feasibility, usability, ethical | (Product Talk, Assumption Testing (2023)) |
| SVPG's four risks: value, usability, feasibility, viability. Cagan says he long understated viability | (SVPG, Build to Learn FAQ (2026)), (SVPG, Strong Opinions Loosely Held (2026)) |
| Riskiest assumptions are important to success AND have little evidence (assumption mapping, from David Bland) | (Product Talk, Assumption Testing (2023)) |
| Phrase assumptions as things that must be true; be specific so the test is small | (Product Talk, Assumption Testing (2023)) |
| Test single assumptions, not whole ideas | (Product Talk, Assumption Testing (2023)) |
| Riskiest Assumption Test: build only what you need to test your biggest unknown, not an MVP | (Hackernoon, Riskiest Assumption Test (2016)) |
| Name each load-bearing assumption as a bet, with a fallback and an early warning signal | (Mind the Product, Three Products Three Failures (2026)) |
| "They love it" and "they will pay" are separate decisions. Enthusiasm is not a receipt | (Mind the Product, Three Products Three Failures (2026)) |

**Taxonomy conflict.** Product Talk lists five types (adds ethical); SVPG lists four. This strategy method uses the five-type list because it is a superset, and notes that viability carries cost, monetization, liability and ethics for AI products (SVPG, Strong Opinions Loosely Held (2026)).

## 7. Opportunity sizing (light touch)

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| TAM is the whole market; SAM is what your model and reach can serve; SOM is what you can realistically win soon | (Product Notebook, Market Sizing (2025)) |
| Top-down is a framing tool; bottom-up from real data (pilots, conversion, price) is preferred for forecasts | (Product Notebook, Market Sizing (2025)) |
| Do not obsess over whether this is "the most important problem"; that question cannot be answered | (SVPG, Strong Opinions Loosely Held (2026)), (SVPG, Build to Learn FAQ (2026)) |

This strategy method asks for a rough bottom-up count only. Full sizing requires separately scoped competitive research.
